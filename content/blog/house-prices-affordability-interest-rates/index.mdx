The usual housing affordability story is simple: house prices rise, wages fail to keep up, and price-to-wage ratios climb.

That story is true, but incomplete.

Most people do not buy a house with cash. They buy with a mortgage, and mortgage affordability depends on both loan size and interest rates. A house at 15% mortgage rates is a very different proposition from the same house at 3%.

This article rebuilds that affordability story using a simple mortgage model over long-run Irish data. It focuses on four linked views: asset price burden, entry payment burden, burden over the life of the mortgage, and lifetime wage share absorbed by the mortgage. A compact PVAF-based model at the end ties interest rates to supportable price-to-wage ratios.

## 1) The Asset Price Story vs Payment Story

Price-to-wage ratios have clearly risen over the long run. But that metric captures the asset-price problem, not the monthly-payment problem.

The first thing to put beside that price-to-wage ratio is the mortgage interest rate. The price line rises over time, while rates move through a very different cycle: high in the 1970s and 1980s, much lower through the 2010s, and rising again more recently.

## 2) The Missing Variable: Interest Rates

In high-rate periods, a fixed monthly budget can support a smaller loan. In lower-rate periods, the same budget supports a larger loan. That mechanical channel matters.

As rates fell over decades, buyers could carry larger mortgages for the same initial payment burden. In a constrained market, that borrowing capacity can be capitalized into higher prices.

This does not imply interest rates explain everything. Supply constraints, regulation, demographics, tax, lending rules, and expectations all matter. But interest rates strongly shape what payment-constrained buyers can bid.

## 3) Burden After Purchase

First-year burden is only one part of the story. Another key question is how quickly mortgage burden fades after purchase.

If nominal wages grow while mortgage payments are fixed (or mostly fixed), mortgage burden can decline significantly over time. Earlier cohorts often saw this debt erosion happen quickly under higher inflation and wage growth regimes.

Newer cohorts can face a different trade-off: lower rates at entry, but slower burden erosion if wage growth is weaker and the initial loan multiple is higher.

## 4) Lifetime Mortgage Burden

We can go further by comparing total mortgage payments over the life of the loan with total earnings over the same period.

- Lifetime burden = total mortgage payments over term / total earnings over same term

This gives a rough measure of the lifetime wage share absorbed by the mortgage — a broader life-cycle affordability measure than the entry year alone.

Again, the result is not a simple upward line. The late 1980s look favourable in this model. The 2000s look extremely expensive. Recent buyers sit in a more uncertain zone, because we do not yet know what future wage growth and interest rates will do. Any estimate for recent cohorts has to be extrapolated.

## Caveats

This is a deliberately simple model and should be read as directional, not definitive.

- It uses gross earnings, not disposable income.
- Average wages and average prices do not represent the same household at all times.
- Household structure changed over time (single-income vs dual-income financing norms).
- Deposit constraints can dominate even when monthly serviceability is acceptable.
- Term assumptions matter materially (for example 25-year vs 35-year loans).
- Recent cohort projections are inherently more uncertain than older completed periods.

## Why This Matters

Affordability is not only about how expensive houses look relative to annual pay. It is about the interaction of prices, rates, wages, inflation, and time.

- Price-to-wage captures the asset-price side.
- Initial burden captures the entry-payment side.
- Cohort and lifetime burden capture the life-course debt side.

## The Maths: PVAF and Price-to-Wage

A simple way to connect interest rates and house-price-to-wage ratios is through the present value annuity factor (PVAF). The PVAF tells us how much loan a given annual mortgage payment can support at a given interest rate over a fixed term. When rates are low, the same payment can support a much larger loan. When rates are high, the same payment supports a much smaller loan.

The standard annuity formula is:

<MathBlock>
  L = A × <MathFraction numerator={<>1 - (1 + r)<sup>-n</sup></>} denominator={<>r</>} />
</MathBlock>

where `L` is the loan amount, `A` is the annual mortgage payment, `r` is the annual interest rate, and `n` is the mortgage term in years. The fraction is the PVAF:

<MathBlock>
  PVAF(r, n) = <MathFraction numerator={<>1 - (1 + r)<sup>-n</sup></>} denominator={<>r</>} />
</MathBlock>

So:

<MathBlock>
  L = A × PVAF(r, n)
</MathBlock>

Divide everything by annual wage `W`. If the buyer can afford to spend a fraction `m` of annual wage on mortgage payments, then `A = mW` and:

<MathBlock>
  <MathFraction numerator={<>L</>} denominator={<>W</>} /> = m × PVAF(r, n)
</MathBlock>

House price `P` is the deposit plus the loan: `P = D + L`. So the price-to-wage ratio is:

<MathBlock>
  <MathFraction numerator={<>P</>} denominator={<>W</>} /> = <MathFraction numerator={<>D</>} denominator={<>W</>} /> + <MathFraction numerator={<>L</>} denominator={<>W</>} />
</MathBlock>

Substituting the mortgage term gives:

<MathBlock>
  <MathFraction numerator={<>P</>} denominator={<>W</>} /> = <MathFraction numerator={<>D</>} denominator={<>W</>} /> + m × PVAF(r, n)
</MathBlock>

Or, more compactly:

<MathBlock>
  Q = d + m × PVAF(r, n)
</MathBlock>

where `Q = P / W` is the house-price-to-wage ratio and `d = D / W` is the deposit measured in years of wages.

That is the fitted model in plain terms. The price-to-wage ratio is treated as the combination of two parts: the deposit a buyer can put down, plus the mortgage their income can service. The mortgage component is shaped by the PVAF. The model is not saying that interest rates explain everything in the housing market. It is saying something narrower: if buyers are constrained by what they can pay each month, then lower interest rates mechanically allow higher price-to-wage ratios.

That gives the broad downward curve in the data. High-rate years tend to support lower price-to-wage ratios. Low-rate years can support much higher ratios. The relationship is not perfect — supply, planning, credit conditions, deposits, loan-to-value limits, demographics, tax, and expectations all matter. But the broad pattern is still useful: lower rates allow higher price-to-wage ratios to be sustained; higher rates compress what buyers can afford.
